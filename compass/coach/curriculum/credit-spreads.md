title: Credit spreads and financial conditions

summary: High-yield spreads are the bond market's fear gauge and often move before stocks.

The **high-yield spread** is the extra yield that riskier "junk" companies pay over Treasuries
(compass uses the ICE BofA US High Yield option-adjusted spread). When lenders worry about
defaults, they demand more and the spread widens.

**Rough ranges:**
- Below about 3.5%: very tight. Credit is easy, and lenders are paid little for risk.
- 3.5–5%: normal.
- 5–7%: stress building.
- Above 7%: real fear. Spreads peaked near 20% in late 2008, about 11% in March 2020 and around 9%
  in the 2015–16 energy bust.

**Why it is useful:** bond investors care about losing money, not about upside, so they often
notice trouble before equity investors do. A fast widening of 1.5 points or more in a few months,
even from a low level, has often come with stock drawdowns. compass flags that separately.

**The NFCI** (Chicago Fed National Financial Conditions Index) bundles more than 100 measures of
money markets, debt, equity and the banking system. Zero is the historical average. Positive
means conditions are tighter than average; negative means looser. It was deeply positive in 2008
and briefly in 2020.

**Reading them together:** very tight spreads together with a stretched CAPE describes a
complacent, late-cycle market, which can last a long time. The useful moment usually comes when
spreads have blown out *and are starting to narrow again*: that has often coincided with the
early part of a recovery, while the news is still bad.
