summary: Why expensive markets return less over a decade but can keep rising for years.

The **cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE)**, popularised by Robert Shiller,
divides the S&P 500's price by its average inflation-adjusted earnings over the past ten years.
Averaging over a decade smooths out the boom and bust in profits, so CAPE shows how expensive the
market is against normal earnings rather than one year's figure.

**Landmarks:**
- Long-run average: about 17.
- About 32 in 1929.
- About 7 in 1982, which began a great 18-year bull market.
- An all-time high of about 44 in December 1999.
- About 38 in late 2021.

**What it predicts well:** the starting CAPE has explained a large share of the variation in the
*next ten years'* real returns. Buying at a CAPE above 30 has historically meant low single-digit
annual returns over the following decade; buying below 10 has meant high returns.

**What it predicts badly:** the next one or two years. CAPE passed 25 in 1996, and the market
roughly doubled before peaking in 2000. If you had frozen all buying at a high CAPE, you would have
held cash for most of the 2010s and 2020s.

**Structural caveats:**
- Today's index is dominated by high-margin, asset-light tech companies.
- Accounting rules changed over time.
- Buybacks have replaced some dividends.

All of these may justify a higher "normal" CAPE than in 1950. Nobody knows how much higher.

**How compass uses it:** compass shows the percentile of today's CAPE against all earlier months
since 1881. The valuation-freeze rule uses that percentile, with hysteresis (freeze above one
level, resume below a lower one), so it doesn't flip on and off every month. The rule's history
view shows how much of the time it would have kept you out, and what the market did meanwhile.
That is the cost of the insurance.
