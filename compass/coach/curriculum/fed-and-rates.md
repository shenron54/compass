The **federal funds rate** is the overnight rate the Federal Reserve targets. It sets the floor for
every other US borrowing cost and, through the dollar, matters far beyond the US. The HKD peg
means Hong Kong rates broadly follow US rates.

**Why rates matter for stocks:**
- A company's value is its future profits discounted back to today. Higher rates mean steeper
  discounting, which hurts long-duration assets most: growth and technology stocks whose profits
  lie far in the future. That is a big reason the Nasdaq-100 fell about 35% in 2022 as the Fed
  raised rates from near zero to over 5%.
- Higher rates also slow borrowing, hiring and spending, with lags of a year or more.

**Cuts come in two kinds:**
- **"Insurance" cuts** (1995, 1998, 2019): the economy is fine and the Fed trims rates to keep it
  that way. Stocks usually did well afterwards.
- **Recession cuts** (2001, 2007–08, 2020): the Fed cuts fast because the economy is breaking.
  Stocks fell for much of the 2001 and 2007–08 cutting cycles.

So "the Fed is cutting" is not automatically bullish. What matters is *why* it is cutting.
Compare it with the credit-spread and labour indicators: cuts while spreads stay tight and jobs
hold up look like insurance; cuts with rising claims and widening spreads look like the other kind.

**How compass reads it:** compass compares the rate today with six months ago and labels it
cutting, hiking or on hold. It's context for the other readings rather than a signal of its own.
