The **200-day moving average** is the average closing price over roughly the past ten months of
trading. Price above it means the market is in an uptrend; below it, a downtrend.

**Why traders watch it:** most of the deepest bear markets (2000–02, 2008) were spent below the
200-day average. Mebane Faber's well-known 2007 study found that holding stocks only while they
were above their 10-month average gave similar long-run returns to buy-and-hold, with much smaller
drawdowns.

**The cost: whipsaws.** In sideways markets the price crosses the average again and again, and a
trend follower sells low and buys back higher. 2015–16, 2018 and late 2022 had several false
breaks. After taxes and trading costs, a lot of the edge disappears.

**Distance from trend** is the other use. When the Nasdaq-100 is more than 15–20% above its 200-day
average, the market has run far ahead of its trend. That's usually followed by slower gains or a
pullback, though not always quickly. compass labels this "extended" and uses it in the trim rule.

**RSI** (Relative Strength Index, J. Welles Wilder, 1978) compares recent up-days with down-days on a
0–100 scale over 14 days:
- Above 70 is "overbought" and below 30 "oversold". Both describe how *fast* the market has moved,
  not where it is going.
- In strong bull markets RSI can stay above 70 for weeks.
- Oversold readings in a long-term uptrend have tended to be good short-term entry points.

**For a long-term investor**, trend is a description of the market, not a trading system. It helps
you see when your drawdown ladder is likely to be used (downtrends), and when a single stock is
far enough above trend to deserve the trim rule's attention.
