---
title: Deploying cash, from lump sum to ladders
order: 10
summary: Lump sum usually wins on average; staged buying wins on regret. Know which you are choosing.
indicators: [ndx_drawdown, vix]
---
You have cash and want to invest it. There are three common approaches:

**1. Lump sum.** Invest everything now. Markets rise more often than they fall, so on average
this wins: a 2012 Vanguard study found lump-sum investing beat spreading the money over 12 months
about two-thirds of the time, in US, UK and Australian data. The cost is regret. Investing it all
just before a −30% drop is painful, and that pain can make you stop investing altogether.

**2. Dollar-cost averaging (DCA).** Invest equal amounts on a schedule, for example monthly. It
loses to lump sum on average, but it smooths your entry price and builds the habit. It is the
right default for money that arrives from salary anyway.

**3. Conditional deployment (a drawdown ladder).** Hold a reserve and deploy it in tranches as the
market falls. This beats DCA when deep drawdowns happen, and leaves cash idle when they don't.
In the 2013–2019 stretch, a −20% ladder sat unused for years while the market doubled. It also
needs discipline at the worst moment: the tranches trigger when the news is terrible.

**Value averaging** is a hybrid: aim for a target portfolio value that rises a fixed amount each
month, and invest more when you are behind target and less (or sell) when you are ahead. It buys
more after falls automatically, but needs a cash buffer.

**A practical combination** many long-term investors use:
- DCA new savings every month, no matter what.
- Keep a separate reserve (in compass, the cash above `cash_floor`) for the ladder.
- Write down the ladder before you need it. compass's job is to tell you when a tranche is reached
  and how much the rule says to deploy. The journal is where you record what you actually did and
  why.

Cash isn't free: it earns roughly the policy rate. When rates are high that is a decent return
while you wait; when they are near zero, waiting costs more.
