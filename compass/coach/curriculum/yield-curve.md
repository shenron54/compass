title: The yield curve
order: 1
summary: Why short rates above long rates have preceded US recessions, and why the timing is loose.

The **yield curve** compares interest rates on government bonds of different maturities. compass
tracks two spreads: the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month bill, and the 10-year minus the
2-year.

**Normally the curve slopes upward.** Lenders want extra pay for tying money up for longer. The
curve **inverts** (the spread goes below zero) when the Fed has pushed short rates up hard and the
bond market expects that to slow the economy enough to force cuts later.

**Track record.** The 10y-3m spread inverted before every US recession since the late 1960s, with
a lead of roughly 6 to 24 months. The New York Fed publishes a recession-probability model built
on this one spread.

**The catches:**
- **Timing is loose.** Stocks often keep rising after an inversion. The curve inverted in 2006 and
  the S&P 500 peaked in October 2007; it inverted in 1998 and again in 2000, and the Nasdaq peaked
  in March 2000.
- **The un-inversion matters more.** Recessions have tended to start close to the point where the
  curve steepens back above zero. The Fed is then cutting quickly because something has broken.
  compass labels this "re-steepening after inversion".
- **It can be wrong.** The 2022–2024 inversion was the longest on record, and at the time this
  lesson was written (mid-2026) it had not been followed by an NBER-dated recession. Big fiscal
  deficits, households locked into cheap fixed-rate mortgages and strong corporate balance sheets
  all blunted the usual channel.

**How to use it.** Treat an inverted or re-steepening curve as a reason to check your downside
plan: is your cash reserve ready, and is concentration within your bands? Don't treat it as a
sell signal. It tells you conditions are late-cycle, not when the turn comes.
